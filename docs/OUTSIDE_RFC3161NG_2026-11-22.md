# Reading rfc3161ng next to our RFC 3161 client (2026-11-22)

**What was read:** `rfc3161ng` 2.1.3, the source package on PyPI (MIT), file `rfc3161ng/api.py`. It was read and not run.

**What it was compared with:** `lib/_rfc3161.js` and `tools/tsa_stamp.js`, written the same day to stamp anchor texts at DigiCert's free time stamp server.

## What they do better

1. **The client can ask for a policy.** `make_timestamp_request` can name a TSA policy (`tsa_policy_id`); ours cannot. We have no use for one yet: DigiCert answers under its default policy, and the policy is reported in our summary.
2. **They take a digest as well as data.** Their API accepts a precomputed digest; ours only stamps a file. Ours is narrower on purpose, since the thing stamped here is always an anchor file.

## What we do better

1. **sha256 only.** Their default hash is sha1 (`hashname or 'sha1'`). Also, `make_timestamp_request(data=…, hashname='sha256')` hashes the data with sha1 anyway (`digest = data_to_digest(data)` drops `hashname`), so the request carries a sha1 digest under a sha256 label.
2. **Every answer is checked against its request.** In their `RemoteTimestamper`, `check_response` returns the token unchecked unless a certificate was configured: no imprint check, no nonce check. The nonce is optional and not made by default. Our tool always sends a random nonce, and always checks that the answer carries this file's sha256 and this request's nonce before anything else.
3. **The chain is checked.** With no certificate given, their `check_timestamp` checks the signature against the first certificate the token itself carries. A token signed by anyone, carrying its own certificate, passes. The certificate is never chained to a root, and only RSA PKCS#1 v1.5 is tried. Our tool has stock `openssl ts -verify` check the signature, the ESS signing-certificate binding and the chain against a CA file.

## The one change made for us, with its tests

Their gap, a signature checked against "whatever certificate is there", had a smaller cousin in our tool. `openssl ts -verify` against the system's TLS root bundle (about 140 roots) proves that some trusted authority signed the token, not that DigiCert did. Our summary never said who signed.

Now `lib/_rfc3161.js` reads the signer's identity from the token's SignerInfo (issuer and serial number) and the certificates the token carries. `tools/tsa_stamp.js` then:
- picks the one certificate whose issuer and serial match byte for byte, from the token or from `--untrusted`;
- reports its subject, issuer and sha256 fingerprint;
- requires its subject to contain `--expect-signer`. The default for DigiCert's server is `O = DigiCert`; a local test server must name whom it expects.

A token that openssl accepts but that another certificate signed is BROKEN, and the summary says who signed it.

The tests are in `test/rfc3161.test.js` (+4). Each runs against a throwaway local authority made with stock `openssl ts -reply`:
- the signer is named in the summary;
- a token that chains to a trusted root but was signed by someone else is BROKEN, even though openssl said `Verification: OK`;
- a decoy certificate named DigiCert in `--untrusted` is not taken as the signer;
- a local server without `--expect-signer` is refused.
